5.01 NOTES - India's Vedic Age

The next phase in Indian history is called the Vedic Age, which lasted from 1500 BCE to 500 BCE. During this time, many Indian traditions were started. The Vedic Age took place in northern and northwestern India.


Many historians believe that the Harappan civilization of the Indus Valley collapsed in about 1500 BCE. This marked the beginning of the Vedic Age. This is when a nomadic tribe entered India through the Khyber Pass. These settlers were nomads from Central Asia, who traveled to northwestern India. When they intermarried with the local people they began to call themselves Aryans. They traveled by horse. The Aryans lived among the people who were already in this region. They became farmers. They were the people that declared cattle sacred and forbid them to be used as food. They grew cotton, which they traded with other civilizations. They made many contributions that led to changes in Indian culture and society.

For example, the Aryans also brought a new language to India. As nomads they had no written language, but in India they developed a written language known as Sanskrit. It has its own alphabet. Sanskrit is known for its clarity and beauty. The Indian people thought it was the language of the gods.
For many years, the people shared ideas and thoughts orally, or by word of mouth. They then used Sanskrit to write down poems, hymns, and other religious ideas. One of the most famous poems ever written was the Bhagavad Gita. Bhagavad was a supreme god, and gita means song. So Bhagavad Gita means "The Song of God."
Bhagavad Gita is the story of the god, Krishna, speaking to his friend, Arjuna. Arjuna is about to go into battle. He is sad because he knows he may be forced to kill some of his relatives who are on the other side. Krishna takes the form of Arjuna’s chariot driver. He tells Arjuna not to be worried. He does this by explaining to Arjuna the nature of humans and the purpose of life.

"What's the Hindu religion?"

The Vedic Age gets its name from the four Vedas, or books of knowledge that were written during this time. The four books are called Rig Veda, Yajur Veda, Sama Veda, and Atharva Veda. These four books record the hymns and prayers of the Hindu religion.

Hinduism grew out of the religious customs of many people over thousands of years. This might explain why Hinduism has so many deities. The Hindu religion gets its name from the Indus River. The one god in Hinduism, from whom all others come, is Brahman. Brahma is one of Brahman's main forms, the one associated with creation. The Hindu religion is not about following any particular religious leader. Instead, Hinduism is about following a way of life. It is about what you do in life instead of what you believe.

The early Hindu worshipers performed animal sacrifices like other ancient civilizations. Then about 300 BCE, the Hindus began to use flowers, incense, music, and prayers as sacrifices.

The Hindu people were very inventive. Like other civilizations, they had a number system based on 10. They made symbols for the numbers one through nine. But they used words for place values. "What Contributions Were Made by the People of the Vedic Age?"

The people of the Vedic Age were farmers. The Aryans’ technology improved farming in India. They dug deep wells and irrigation systems to water their fields. They invented an iron plow to help clear India’s many jungles. They planted seeds and used animals to plow the fields. They grew barley, corn, and rice. They had a short growing season.

Farming was not an easy job for these people. There were seasons of floods and long periods without any rain. Insects and other animals would eat the seeds and crops. As farmers, they knew the importance of giving back to the soil. They were the first to use the manure of their cattle and oxen to enrich the soil. Ownership of land became important. Farmers had to protect and claim their fields. Goats, sheep, and bulls were also part of the farmers’ lives. These animals were raised to feed the farmer and his family. They were also used for their wool and hides.<>

India’s land is rich with many minerals. Iron, copper, silver, and gold are some of the minerals found in India. It is no surprise that the people who lived here learned a lot about metals.

Metallurgy is the science of how metals work. This form of technology includes how to separate metal from its surrounding materials. Making metals pure and combining metals are also parts of metallurgy. The early metallurgists of India used their knowledge to make tools and art. Because the people here lived peacefully, they did not need to make many swords or shields.

"What Were the Benefits and Drawbacks of Trade?"

During this time the lack of a good road system might have hampered trade, but river navigation existed. This allowed trade among civilizations both locally and regionally. Coins were not available at this time so this meant that they traded by bartering.

Items that were traded included crops and cattle. There was little need for craft products. Some people lived their entire life never leaving the place of their birth.

5.02 NOTES - Hinduism

"What is Hinduism?"

Hinduism is the largest religion in India today. It is the third largest in the world. Eighty percent of Indians are Hindu. Modern-day Hinduism developed over many centuries. It began with the people of the Indus Valley. Eventually, it branched into many forms. The Hindu religion has spread throughout Asia and the world.

"How did Hinduism develop and spread in India?"

"Give me more elaboration on the Caste System." One of the beliefs of the Hindu religion is the caste system. The caste system is a social structure where a person’s place in society is determined by his or her job. People are born into their caste, meaning that their birth determines the jobs they can have. The caste system in Hinduism originated with the Aryan people and their religion. In the Aryan caste system, there were thousands of castes, one for each job a person could have. Aryans organized these castes in order of importance. The Hindus grouped these thousands of castes into four categories. The first category was called the Brahmin. The Brahmin were the priests and academics. The second was called the Kshatriya, or the warriors and kings. The third was called the Vaishya, or landowners, farmers, and merchants. The fourth was called the Shudra, or the commoners, peasants and servants. The Shudra were the lowest class in the caste system. About twenty percent of Hindus in India did not belong to the caste system. Instead, they formed a fifth class which was located beneath the Shudra. They were called Untouchables. The Untouchables were the lowest people in society. The caste system was very oppressive. In many cases, Hindus could not eat with or even speak to members of other castes. Marrying someone of another caste was forbidden. For a long time, Hindus believed that people deserved to be in whatever caste they belonged, and that a person’s caste placement was the result of actions during previous lives. Hindus believe in one central god, who is called Brahma. However, they also believe that Brahma has many different forms. For this reason, Hindus have millions of gods who they worship separately. The main three gods are Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva. They represent a unit of three gods who complete the cycle of creation, protection, and destruction. This cycle is repeated again and again.

5.03 NOTES - Buddhism

"What's Buddhism?"

Buddhism is a philosophy. It is also a religion. It was founded in ancient India by Siddhartha Gautama. Gautama became known as the Buddha. He is a leader of Buddhism in the country of Tibet.

Buddhist tradition says that Siddhartha Gautama learned what life is all about by meditating. Then, when he was named Buddha, he spent the rest of his life teaching others.

The Buddha lived in India from about 563 – 483 BCE. At this time, the main belief system in India was Brahmanism. Times began to change as many of the Indian beliefs of Brahmanism slowly turned into a religion called Hinduism. Hinduism continued on with the main beliefs of Brahmanism and expanded those beliefs over time. But there were people in India who did not believe in Brahmanism. They did not approve of the complex rituals and animal sacrifices of Brahmanism that continued in Hinduism. So at the same time that Hinduism expanded, the people of India who did not believe in Brahmanism formed their own religions. One of these ways was Buddhism. Buddhists did not believe in the Hindu gods and goddesses or in any godlike being. Neither did they agree with the caste system.

Another religion, Jainism, also arose during this time. Its followers, Jains, led an ascetic lifestyle. They believed in ahimsa, or nonviolence. The Jains refused to sacrifice animals. In fact, they believed that all living creatures have a soul. For this reason, Jains were vegetarians. They swept the path in front of them to avoid stepping on bugs. They would not kill a fly. Buddhists, too, were vegetarians. They also practiced nonviolence but not to the extreme degree that the Jains did. Followers of the two religions shared other beliefs as well. Like Hindus, they believed in reincarnation. They also believed in karma.

"How'd Siddhartha Gautama Become the Buddha?"

According to Buddhist tradition, Siddhartha Gautama began his life as a prince. He grew up in a palace, isolated from common people. At the age of 29, Siddhartha decided to explore the world outside the palace gates. What he witnessed had a strong impact on him. Gautama took four trips outside the palace. On his trips, Gautama saw an old crippled man, a sick man, and a dead man. Their suffering depressed him. On his fourth trip, he came upon a holy man. This man had no possessions. Yet he appeared to be content—satisfied with his way of life.

Seeing those four men prompted Gautama to take action. He abandoned his princely life to become an ascetic. For seven years, Gautama wandered across India in search of the end of suffering. He studied with Hindu scholars. That did not get him the answers he was looking for. He tried fasting, or going without food. That did not work, and it nearly killed him.

Then, one day, Gautama sat down under a fig tree, known as the Bodhi tree. He began to meditate. He would stay there for 49 days. When he finished, he claimed to have found the steps to end suffering. He was now called the Buddha—the Enlightened One- by his followers.

"What'd the Buddha teach?"

The Buddha believed that a life of moderation was important. He had found enlightenment by this method. Now he wanted to show others how to do the same thing. Once again he began to travel across India. This time, through his teachings, he spread a message of awakening. The Buddha gathered a community of followers through the act of teaching people what he understood to be the path of awakening.

Under the fig tree, the Buddha had realized some important things about the human condition. Buddhists call them the Four Noble Truths. They have to do with suffering, its causes, and how to overcome it. The way to overcome suffering, the Buddha said, is by following the Eightfold Path.Buddhists believe in reincarnation. The Buddha said that the goal of life should be to escape the endless cycle of rebirth and end personal suffering. Buddhists call this goal nirvana. The Buddha taught that following the Eightfold Path was the way to achieve nirvana.

5.04 NOTES - The Mauryan Empire

"What was the Mauryan Empire?"

Different Aryan kings competed for power in ancient India. Around 550 BCE, the kingdom of Magadha rose to power. By the fourth century BCE, it included most of northern India.

The conqueror Alexander the Great entered India in 327–326 BCE. He triumphed over the Magadha kingdom. However, he did not want to stay in the territory he had won. Instead, he left behind a force to occupy the land. He moved on, leaving political unrest in the area.

In 321 BCE, Chandragupta Maurya took over the former Magadha kingdom. It became the center of the Mauryan Empire. Chandragupta wished to expand his land further. He worked his way westward. In 305–303 BCE, he defeated the troops Alexander had left behind.

By 297 BCE, Chandragupta controlled most of Afghanistan and Baluchistan. He also controlled the Indus Valley. He united the northern provinces. He made the people in this area his subjects. Then he turned his rule over to his son Bindusara. Bindusara extended the empire southward.

The third emperor was Asoka. Asoka brought all of India, except the far south, into the empire. He came to power in 268 BCE. In 261 BCE, he fought a long, terrible war. This war increased the amount of land in the Mauryan Empire. It also expanded the religion of Buddhism.

"What was the Mauryan Empire Government like?"

The first Mauryan ruler, Chandragupta, formed a highly organized bureaucracy. This helped the government run smoothly. Roads and harbors were built to help trade grow. Royal officials collected taxes. Factories and shipyards were owned by the government. Royal courts assisted those who needed justice. The rule of law was effective. It was also harsh. Chandragupta had a force of secret police to spy on people. He feared that enemies would take his power. Because of this fear, he trained a group of women warriors to guard his palace.

The third Mauryan emperor, Asoka, is the most well known. He was good at military strategy, or using armed forces during war. His army was strong and well-organized. The chain of command was clear. Soldiers were well-paid and well-trained. They even used training manuals! Asoka used of a force of 9,000 elephants. These beasts frightened opponents with their great size. They also trampled and destroyed the enemy’s lands.

Early in Asoka's rule, he wished to seize India's southern trade routes. These routes were controlled by the Kingdom of Kalinga. They were Asoka's last remaining competition in India. He set out to take over Kalinga through a brutal war. He won the war but at a great loss of life.

"How were Buddhism and Asoka connected?"

As a result of war, Asoka witnessed great suffering. He wrote: “a hundred thousand people deported … and many times that number perished.” This suffering affected him greatly. Asoka converted to Buddhism. From this point on, he rejected violence. He gave up eating most types of meat. He also limited Hindu animal sacrifices. Asoka made Buddhism the official religion of the Mauryan Empire. He spread Buddhism by sending missionaries and promoting tolerance to other religions.

"How'd religions affect India?"

Hinduism had been in India for a long time before the Mauryan Empire. Hindu priests spoke Sanskrit when they conducted sacrifices. Not many Indian people spoke Sanskrit at this time. This caused a divide between the priests and the people. The people felt distant from the gods. As a result, many turned to Buddhism.

Buddhism grew from Hinduism. Both beliefs preach ahimsa, or nonviolence. Both believe in karma and dharma. They also believe in a cycle of rebirth. The religions differ in some ways, too. Buddhism directs people to find truth on their own. Meditation is considered a way to do this. Buddhists also reject the caste system. They offer the hope of nirvana to all.

Asoka was determined to rule through moral example. He ordered that stone pillars be set up throughout India. These pillars promised a just government and offered advice on how to behave. He built hospitals and Buddhist shrines. He created roads and resting places for travelers. These good works brought peace and prosperity to his people. They also helped to unite a large land with many different types of people.

Asoka helped spread Buddhism across India. Buddhism then spread to other parts of Asia. It continues to thrive across Asia today.

"How'd the Mauryan Empire end?"

The power of the Mauryan Empire declined after Asoka died in around 232 BCE. By 185 BCE, India was no longer united. Rival princes once again fought for power. Many kingdoms grew up across the land. Over time, Hindu and Buddhist beliefs from the north worked southward. Buildings such as the stupa shown here survived. A stupa is usually shaped like a bell or a hemisphere. They can be small or very large. They are shrines for Buddhists.
