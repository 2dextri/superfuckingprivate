FIGURATIVE LANGUAGE (Important)

Words in italic and bold show the actual example!

  • Alliteration: a repetition of the beginning consonant sounds in nearby words to create rhythm.

  • Example: "Let's not do that again," Medkit said, steadily.

  • Hyperbole: an exaggeration of a claim or statement.

  • Example: "Let's not do that again," Medkit spoke wistfully, before proceeding to shooting the enemy a million times.

  • Idiom: an expression that cannot be understood from the meaning of its separate words, but must be learned as a whole.

  • Example: "Let's not do that again," Medkit spoke wistfully, biting the bullet. He moved towards the enemy ready for their phinisher, but he made his way to a dead teammate to resurrect them.

  • Personification: a figure of speech in which nonliving or nonhuman things are given human characteristics or abilities.

  • Example: "Let's not do that again," Medkit spoke wistfully, before reloading his revolver. It screeched as he loaded another round of ammunition into it.

  • Onomatopoeia: a word that imitates a sound.

  • Example: 'Ding!' A ping played from their horns, signaling their phinisher was ready.

  • Metaphor: a figure of speech that compares two unrelated things. No I will not write phighting.

  • Example: "Wow, this beat is genuine dogshit," Rust muttered while looking at the notes of zero_one's DAW.

  • Simile: basically metaphor, but it uses "like" or "as".

  • Example: "Let's not do that again," Medkit spoke wistfully, her words sharp as a sword.

04.01 ~ Making Arguments That Matter

  • Steps to making a good argument:

  1. Examine a question that has more than one side. For example: Are double healers good?

  2. Take a firm stance on the subject. Double healers are bad, because..

  3. Present evidence to support your opinion. Double healers are bad because they are nerfed and are much weaker.

  4. After making your points, ask your audience to take action. I don't know how to do this, but I included it for the notes anyways.

Argumentative essay structure example:

Introductory Paragraph

  • Explains the importance of an issue

  • Presents your claim/position

2 or 3 Body Paragraphs

  • Offer reasons for your position

  • Provide support from valid and reliable sources

  • Provie elaboration to expand on the evidence

Conclusion Paragraph

  • Recaps your ideas

  • Makes a call to action

An effective claim HAS to take a clear position on a debatable topic, does not state emotions or feelings, is written in third-person, and shows that the purpose is to argue, not to inform.

04.02 ~ Organize and Introduce Your Argument

When you write an argumentative essay, look at it as a whole and ask yourself these:

  • What does your topic address? Your argumentative essay must be clear enough for someone who is uninformed to understand.

  • Why should your audience care? Give a reason for people to pay attention.

Remember that if people do not understand your topic, they may stop reading. They may also stop if you give them a weak or unrelated reason to care.

A strong claim is:

  • Specific

  • Focused on the topic question

  • Clear and understandable

  • Written in third-person

  • Devoid of joy and emotions and personal opinions

A weak claim is:

  • Vague or general

  • Unfocused, does not express a clear stance

  • Confusing

  • Written in first/second person (Consists of pronouns like "I" or "You".)

  • Full of emotion and joy and opinions.

Researching

When creating a solid claim, you need support from reliable sources. You can usually find them on the internet, but you must consider that not every site is reliable or trustworthy. There are four things you must always consider:

  • Look for where it comes from. If the author of the source is an expert, then the source could be reliable. If the source is old and related to a topic that changes often (like old Crossroads), it may not be reliable.

  • Look for the reason why it exists. If the goal is to entertain, it won't include the reliable facts you need. If it is to persuade, it may not be reliable. If it is to inform, it could be reliable. Still, you should look at the site as a whole, the URL and origin to make sure.

  • Look for the opinions. If a source is biased, it has an opinion about something or favors one thing over another. A source with a bias may only give facts and information that support author opinions. Emotional language like "Everyone should" or similar are signs of bias. These are NOT reliable.

  • Look for the URL above. Look at the suffix.

  1. .gov, .edu - Usually very reliable.

  2. .org, other - Sometimes reliable.

  3. .com, .net - Sometimes unreliable.

04.03 ~ Show Some Support

Elaborative Techniques

  • Explaination: Provide more specific information about the details or evidence of a certain thing. This can be done by explaining the who, what, when, where, how or why; by explaining a cause and/or effect; or explaining how two things are similar or different.

  • Definition: Provide meanings of unfamiliar words or concepts.

  • Description: "Paint a picture" in the reader's mind with creative words and figurative language.

Examples:

"Medkit is a healer in the combat indie game "PHIGHTING!". It is a game about fictional beings that resemble demons called "Inphernals" who participate in combat-related tournaments in a world that consists of four segments called "Factions". << Explaination, explains the game and gives details"

"Inphernals are fictional demons who are born in a specific faction. A gear is assigned to them at spawn. << Definition, explains unfamiliar concept of inphernals"

"The game "PHIGHTING!" takes place in a world with 7 deities. In the PHIGHT! Lobby, it consists of a green and blue screen behind one of the characters, Zuka. There are several grey slopes near the screens, along with two test dummies, Rojer and Roger, where you can test out the Phighter's abilities."

04.04 ~ Your Argumentative Rough Draft

Stages of a closing paragraph:

  1. Restate the claim, to keep your intentions and reasons clear.

  2. Call Of Action: Finish your argument by giving readers an action item. Tell them what they can do to resolve an issue.

  3. Restate Importance: Remind readers of your topic and why they should care. You can also do this by summarizing the main reasons to support your claim, but be sure to explain it in a unique way to not sound repetitive.

  4. Closing Transition: Use a closing transition to begin your final paragraph.

04.07 ~ Misguided Arguments

Words in italic and bold show the actual example!

If there are any arguments that consist of emotional words or referencing false things, they are known as logical fallacies that drastically weaken any argument, similar to the double healer curse.

  • Ad Hominem: This can be identified when someone rejects or attacks someone's motives, character, or individual traits instead of analyzing the content or validity of the argument.

  • Example: If you don't like double healers, you just don't care about splitting the healing for another teammate in need.

  • Bandwagon Fallacy: A fallacy where someone claims an idea, belief or action is true or justifiable because a lot of people believe in it or do it.

  • Example: Double healers are okay, lots of people just like to choose Medkit, Vinestaff or Coil. How bad could it be?

  • Red Herring: A fact, idea or clue that takes people's attention away from the central point, main issue or real solution.

  • Example: Double healers are bad and you don't want me picking Vinestaff? What? We have a bigger issue, it's that our Banhammer is on low!

  • Slippery Slope: When someone claims that a small action will trigger a chain reaction, ending in a terrible outcome.

  • Example: Double healers.. if there's only one healer, our tank will die because YOU were too busy healing the Slingshot on 1 HP while our tank was on 100 and then they'll be chased and die and then the other team gets on point and then we lose by ONE point!

  • Strawman: When someone exaggerates, oversimplifies or distorts someone else's argument to make it easier to attack.

  • Example: You don't want double healers? Not even triple? I can't believe you'd want to put our tanks in danger!

05.03 ~ Nice to Meet You

There are many ways for characterizing characters. Direct and indirect characterization are different things, but indirect is more important and powerful because it shows rather than tells, requiring the reader to look at a character's personality instead of being spoon-fed labels from the narrator. For example:

  • "It had been a day since Sword last saw Medkit. He'd been going outside to ask people where and when they last saw Medkit, but there was no help. Sword was sad." << Direct characterization. This is acceptable, but indirect characterization is more powerful.

  • "It had been a day since Sword last saw medkit. He'd been going outside to ask people where and when they last saw Medkit, but there was no help. She kept overthinking the way back home — What could've happened to Medkit?" << Indirect characterization. This leaves Sword's status SOMEWHAT ambiguous. If you squinted and actually focused, this shows that Sword is concerned and overthinking about Medkit's current whereabouts.

Conflict Types

  • Character vs. Self: This conflict is internal. The protagonist struggles with a decision they have to make or feelings they have about themselves.

  • Character vs. Society: This conflict is external. The protagonist may have a different opinion from the majority of the society or may want to change society in some way.

  • Character vs. Nature: This is an external conflict. This conflict occurs when a character faces resistance from a natural force. A character may have to survive or recover from a storm, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

  • Character vs. Character: This conflict is external. The protagonist disagrees with one or more characters about what to do, what is right, or how to do something.

05.04 ~ Get Versed in Verbals

Types of verbals:

  • Gerund: a verbal that acts as a noun and always ends with -ing.

  • Infinitive: a verbal that acts as a noun, adjective, or adverb and always starts with "to".

  • Participle: a verbal that acts like an adjective and ends in -ing, -ed, -d, -en, -n, -t.

Examples of gerunds, actual examples in bold and italic.

"The bullets from Medkit's gun were running low." << The verb here is the word "run".

Examples of infinitives:

"ROCKET!! MEDKIT'S DEAD!! WE HAVE TO FIND THEM!!" << The verb here is the word "find".

Note that participles can:

  • Always act like adjectives

  • Can be past or present

  • End in -ing, -ed, -d, -t, -en, or -n

06.01 ~ Mix It Up

  • Long sentences can slow a story down, reflect a character's stream of thinking, and convey a certain mood. Short sentences can speed a story up as well.

  • Dashes, commas, semicolons, and colons can speed up or slow down a sentence. They can reflect the mood of a narrator or character, depending if the story is 1st person or 3rd person. Punctuation can also tell the reader how to read the sentence, like an em dash (—) can show a short pause in dialogue. It also shows when there is emphasis.

  • Colons can list information and show what comes next. Semicolons though, connects two separate complete sentences. Examples:

"For the Multiplayer Tour, the tombsonas visited a number of countries: North America, Europe, and South America."

Semicolon usage example:

(NOTE THAT THE TWO SENTENCE MUST AT LEAST BE CLOSELY RELATED IN THOUGHT)

"zero_one made mac and cheese for dinner; they were starving."

Colons can also emphasize sentences and words more expressively as well. Examples:

"After hours of working on a new invention, Tesla put their head on her desk and closed their eyes: he had never felt so tired."

Note that you can ABSOLUTELY NOT capitalize the initial letter of any word if it isn't a name or the first-person pronoun I.

06.02 ~ Planning a Narrative

Narrative Types

  • Personal: Personal narratives reflect the author's life, it is usually an autobiography.

  • Fictional: Fictional narratives tells stories that didn't happen. They can be set in any setting and have made-up characters who do not reference anybody in reality.

Narrative Purposes

  • Inspire: These narratives can make the reader feel like they can accomplish or achieve something. It can motivate the reader to get through a tough situation in life.

  • Teach: This gives the reader an idea or lesson about how they should live their life and learn from an event without experiencing it for themselves. Unlike inspiring narratives, it has not happened to the reader yet.

  • Inform: This tells the story of a real person or event. For example, a biography. This gives the reader a better understanding of a topic they may not know much about.

  • Persuade: This tells a story to convince the reader to act or believe a certain way. Usually, it makes an emotional connection through the story and the characters to persuade.

  • Entertain: These narratives usually tell a story that is interesting and funny that captures the reader's attention, usually containing an absurd or nonsensical plot.

Some narratives may have a mix of these purposes.

06.04 ~ Writing a Noteworthy Narrative

Narrative Techniques:

  • Dialogue: words and conversations between characters or just one. It can be used to advance a plot, show the character's personality or develop a character.

  • Flashback: shows a past memory to show a past event or significant occurrence to tell the reader what happened, a reason something happened, or a reminder.

  • Foreshadowing: a subtle something to create suspense.

  • Example, since it might be hard to recognize:

"Kris walked up to the monster. "This festival is so fun! Value, value, value!" They spoke. The next day, they met W.D Gaster through a fun value."

  • Pacing: it really depends. Pacing can be shown through shorter or longer sentences, usually to create tension, a mood or tone. For example, longer sentences make the story feel slower, which is also an influence to pacing.

(Quick note about dialogue: tags can change the tone of dialogue, so make sure to keep them clear. For example:)

""You did great," Medkit spoke to a teammate, smiling faintly." << This shows Medkit being proud of their teammate :)

""You did great," Medkit spoke to a teammate, their words followed with a groan of sarcasm." << Medkit is mad at her teammate :(

""You did great," Medkit spoke to a teammate." << Usually the latter would seem clearer. This standalone sentence does not show if Medkit is mad at the teammate or proud. :/

06.05 ~ The Writer's Craft

Authors can narrate things differently to show different point of views. For example:

  • Medkit shot at one of their teammates. Another teammate, Boombox, was on ever lower health — from afar, the opposing Hyperlaser aimed at Boombox, who was on the brink of death and fired their eviscerating ray. Medkit was too late to heal Boombox, but she'd respawn anyway.. Plus, Medkit healed a tank (which contributes more to the point), so it was probably worth the sacrifice. << Focuses on Medkit's POV, showing that he thought he did something justifiable.

  • "Fucking useless!!!!!!!!!!!" Boombox thought before they died, growing a grudge on Medkit's lack of reliability. << Focuses on Boombox's POV, showing that he thought Medkit was a shit teammate.

These are also useful to show the full pictures from both phighters.

Sensory Language/Imagery

Describing what your characters see, hear, smell, taste, and feel shows the reader to imagine those and experience the story in a way that feels like they're with the characters. Examples:

  • Sight

  • Like every day in Crossroads, it was busy, crowded with inphernals and phighters. Lights from buildings glowed bright from above.

  • Sound

  • Like every day in Crossroads, it was full of chatter and unpredictable sounds. The sound of conversations bleeding together from everywhere rang against Medkit's ears.

  • Smell

  • The smell of smoke and charred objects wafted through the air of the fallen state of Crossroads.

  • Taste

  • During Crossroads Must Fall, Rocket grabbed one of Flipside's energy drinks before taking a full swig of it. It was acidic and had a sweet carbonated aftertaste.

  • Touch

  • The bullet grazed against Sword's arm, leaving a spontaneous burning sensation lingering on her arm.

06.06 ~ Dare To Compare

Note that it's important to also add adjectives or figurative language to sentences instead of constantly adding sensory imagery. Some variants of imagery:

  • "ROCKET!! MEDKIT'S DEAD!!" Sword said. << This genuinely sucks. It does not show how Sword is feeling or emphasizes on their panic.

  • "My dearest friend, Rocket Launcher — I truly regret to inform you that our dear friend, First Aid Kit, has not been seen ereyesterday or overmorrow of ereyesterday. My deepest condolences to their well-being!" Sword spoke. << Despite how fancy your words are, this makes it feel like Sword was the one who made Medkit go missing. Short sentences are OK as long as it has soul, this is too long.

  • "ROCKET!! MEDKIT'S DEAD!!" Sword tightly gripped onto Rocket's shoulders, her knuckles turning white. His voice raised with every word, laced in panic. << Valid! This shows sensory language and gives the reader a good sense on what Sword is feeling.

  • "ROCKET!! MEDKIT'S DEAD!!" Sword yelled in panic. << Also valid, it uses adjectives instead of sensory image which makes the pacing faster and clearly shows Sword's panic. Using shorter sentences is a narrative technique used to show unclear or spontaneous thoughts.

  • "ROCKET!! MEDKIT'S DEAD!!" Sword said scaredly. << The dialogue is OK, but the way you describe Sword does not make his panic clear.

Also, you can use superlatives, which are adjectives/adverbs to show something that has the highest or lowest degree of a quality compared to something. For example:

  • "Yeah, I agree. Medkit was the worst teammate ever." << The word worst is an irregular superlative, meaning the superlative doesn't usually end with the words "est" or start with "most/more/less/least".

  • "Yeah, I agree. Medkit was the fastest teammate we had." << The word fastest is a superlative, too.

  • "Yeah, I agree. Medkit was the most difficult to keep alive." << The word "most difficult" is a longer variant of a superlative.
